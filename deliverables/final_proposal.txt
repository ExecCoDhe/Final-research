Abstract. A financial claim may agree with a genuine filed number yet be wrong for the requested
reporting context. Existing financial verifiers already ground XBRL facts, check arithmetic, and
abstain; temporal fact-checking and ambiguous-evidence benchmarks also exist. The remaining
research opportunity is narrower: an auditable comparison of verification under explicitly different
filing-authority policies. We propose a benchmark built from authentic original and corrective
disclosures, with researcher-authored paired claims and exact evidence provenance. The same
evidence packet is evaluated under original-as-filed, historical-as-known, and latest-known policies.
A transparent resolver selects only facts eligible at the cutoff and supersedes them only through
documented, fact-scoped correction links. It returns supported, contradicted, or underdetermined
verdicts, while operational refusals are recorded separately. The primary comparison pits this
resolver against strong period-aware symbolic and latest-filing baselines using identical evidence.
Issuer-disjoint splits, a locked test set, issuer-level inference, and a coverage-sensitive error analysis
prevent gains from retrieval advantages, duplicated claims, or blanket abstention. The design
targets 600 claims across 50 issuers, conditional on acquisition and annotation gates, and needs no
model training or GPU for its core experiment. Contributions are a policy-conditioned evaluation
protocol, provenance-linked annotations, and an empirical test of a simple metadata intervention;
superior performance and global novelty remain hypotheses. A negative result would establish
when ordinary structured verification is suﬀicient.
Keywords: financial claim verification; filing amendments; temporal provenance; XBRL; selective
prediction; benchmark validity; evidence authority.

2. Motivation and problem statement

The problem is selecting the right version of evidence. Consider a claim about a company’s
annual revenue. An original disclosure and a later correction can supply different values for the
same period. A statement about what the original filing said may remain historically accurate after
the correction. A statement about the corrected accounts can require the later value. Matching the
number, fiscal-year label, and company is therefore insuﬀicient when the requested authority policy
differs. This follows from distinguishing reporting time from evidence availability, a distinction
grounded in temporal databases [6] and SEC filing metadata [1].
Financial verification itself is established. VeriFin checks source-grounded numerical constraints;
FinGround verifies typed claims and explicitly defaults to the latest filing when restated figures
conflict; FinDVer evaluates document-grounded financial claims [10, 11, 12]. Our question is
whether those controls support historically conditioned claims, and whether a small, inspectable
change in authority resolution supplies measurable value.

  Problem statement. Given a claim, a disclosure cutoff, an explicit authority policy, and the
  same set of candidate financial facts supplied to every method, determine the verdict and its
  evidence lineage. Measure errors caused specifically by choosing an unavailable, superseded,
  or incorrectly scoped disclosure.


2.1 Regulatory motivation without overclaiming

ECOA and Regulation B. For covered credit decisions, adverse-action notifications must give
specific principal reasons or the prescribed right to obtain them; the reasons must accurately
describe factors actually considered. The oﬀicial interpretation of 12 CFR 1002.9(b)(2) does not
mandate a number of reasons and says more than four is unlikely to help. It does not require
SHAP, a four-feature model, or a recourse plan [3].
FCRA. The adverse-action and credit-score disclosure provisions have different triggers and con-
tent. Where the applicable score disclosure is required, the key adverse score factors are generally
limited to four, or five when inquiries are a key factor. Those score factors do not replace ECOA’s
reasons for the decision; FCRA also provides reporting, dispute, and disclosure rights. Neither
cited provision guarantees future approval after a suggested action [4, 3].
EU AI Act. Annex III 5(b) lists systems evaluating natural persons’ creditworthiness or credit
scores, with an exception for financial-fraud detection. This is not an exemption from the entire
Act. Article 86 provides qualified rights to explanations of certain consequential decisions; it does
not require an algorithmic recourse plan. A corporate-filing research verifier is not automatically
a creditworthiness system [5]. These laws motivate accuracy and traceability in their applicable
settings; they do not legally mandate this benchmark. Current transition-date inconsistencies in
retrieved oﬀicial pages are recorded in Section 14 and Appendix C.

3. Background from first principles

3.1 A financial number needs a context

A financial fact is more than a value. “Revenue: 12.0” is incomplete without the entity, currency
and scale, reporting interval, accounting concept, consolidation scope, and source. Revenue is a
duration quantity; a cash balance is an instant quantity. A quarter’s revenue and year-to-date
revenue can end on the same date while representing different intervals. Different figures can both
be correct. Equal figures can describe different concepts. SEC’s Frames interface deliberately
approximates calendar periods and must not be used as an exact fiscal-period identity test [1].
XBRL attaches machine-readable concepts, units, and contexts to financial facts. Inline XBRL
embeds tags in a human-readable filing. An SEC accession identifies a submission. Company Facts
is an aggregation interface, not a complete representation of every company-specific or dimensional
fact: its documented scope includes non-custom taxonomies and facts applying to the entire filing
entity [1]. The source filing and its notes remain necessary when context or correction scope is
ambiguous.

3.2 Three clocks and three questions

The reporting interval describes the period of economic activity. The availability time describes
when a disclosure could have been known. The acquisition time describes when the researcher
retrieved it. These clocks are different. A current download can contain a correction to an old
reporting period; the correction must not enter a historical evidence set before its availability time.
This adapts established temporal-data concepts rather than inventing a new notion of time [6].

“Latest” is meaningful only with a cutoff and correction scope. A later administrative amendment

3.3 Verification, uncertainty, and the research unit

A verifier judges whether a specified claim follows from permitted evidence. It does not establish
that the company’s underlying accounts are economically correct. A faithfully extracted but
erroneous filing can support a claim about what was reported. Accounting truth, legal materiality,
and investment suitability are separate questions outside this study.
The proposed gold labels are S, supported; C, contradicted; and U, underdetermined by the au-
thorized context. A missing document is not evidence of falsity. Nor is finding one matching
number enough when equally admissible evidence supports another interpretation. AmbiFC al-
ready demonstrates the need to represent interpretive disagreement; VeriFin and FinVerBench
already recognize unavailable or incomplete support [9, 10, 13].
An operational refusal, timeout, or invalid response receives a separate marker R. A system that
cannot parse an otherwise answerable claim has failed; it has not discovered that the claim is
semantically underdetermined. This distinction prevents a method from gaining apparent safety
by avoiding the diﬀicult cases.

3.4 Why controlled comparisons matter

Suppose one system sees an original filing and another sees the original plus a correction. A
performance difference cannot be attributed solely to verification. Our primary experiment sup-
plies identical evidence packets, policy instructions, and output options. FinGround already uses
retrieval-equalized evaluation, so evidence parity is a necessary control rather than a novel method
[11].
Claims sharing a filing also share extraction errors, concepts, and annotation decisions. Treating
600 related claims as 600 independent observations would overstate precision. We aggregate paired
method differences by issuer, keep related disclosures in one split, and report the actual number
of independent units assumed by each test. The concern is analogous to comparing classifiers over
dependent folds rather than independent datasets [29, 28].

3.5 A newcomer’s worked example

Synthetic setup. Filing A says revenue is 12.0 billion. Filing B, available later, explicitly corrects
it to 11.6 billion. Both are reported to the nearest 0.1 billion. Thus their rounding intervals are
approximately [11.95, 12.05] and [11.55, 11.65], respectively.
For the claim “accession A reported 12.0 billion,” the first interval supports the claim. For “the
corrected figure known after B is 12.0 billion,” the authorized interval does not overlap the claim’s
interval, so it is contradicted. Without a specified authority policy, admissible interpretations can
disagree, producing U. The difference is 0.4 billion, or approximately 100(0.4/12.0) = 3.333% of
the original figure. This arithmetic illustrates semantics; it is not an observed restatement, effect
size, or financial loss.

4.2 Stream I: grounded financial verification

VeriFin (Hall, Shome, and Eiers, 2026). Its contribution is an acceptance-control pipeline
that independently grounds operands in XBRL, authorizes a calculation from source-backed defini-
tions, and checks constraints using Z3. It records concept, period, unit, accession, and source fact,
and can return Verified, Violated, or Abstain. The inspected v2 uses XBRLFiling and numerical
subsets of FinanceBench and TAT-DQA [10].
Its assumptions are consequential: the intended concept and formula must be identifiable, the
grounded facts must be appropriate, and precision must be represented correctly. Solver correctness
is conditional on those premises. Zero observed false accepts in the authors’ finite evaluations is
not a universal guarantee. The paper already addresses wrong periods and evidence insuﬀiciency,
so neither exact-date filtering nor a third label is a credible standalone contribution for us.
Relation to the proposal. The primary reference is a source-grounded structured verifier with
the same authorized arithmetic as our method. Our only principal addition is explicit interpreta-
tion of historical authority across authentic versions. The experiment must ask whether the best
ordinary resolver already handles the cases; a favorable comparison with number matching or a
generic LLM is insuﬀicient.
FinGround (Guo, Wu, and Yiu, 2026). FinGround combines finance-aware retrieval, typed
atomic verification, and regeneration with citations. Its retrieval-equalized comparison is directly
relevant to experimental fairness. Crucially, §3.3 says that when restated figures conflict it defaults
to the most recent filing date and flags the conflict. Its Appendix J identifies restated figures as a
source of false positives [11].
This is closer prior art than P2’s summary implies. It assumes that recent-filing preference is
an appropriate default for the requested answer. That assumption is reasonable for some latest-
known questions and potentially wrong for original-as-filed or historical-cutoff questions. We will
implement a named latest-only baseline and a cutoff-aware latest baseline, instead of claiming that
financial version conflicts have never been considered.
FinDVer (Zhao et al., 2024). The inspected paper formulates explainable entailment/refutation
against a supplied long financial document, with information-extraction, numerical-reasoning, and
knowledge-intensive subsets. Evidence and reasoning annotations make it an important standard
for label traceability [12]. A supplied source document defines its authority context; it is not an
evaluated cross-version authority policy in the inspected formulation.
The published PDF describes 2,100 examples, split into 600 development and 1,500 test cases,
and 19 evaluated models. Its landing abstract instead reports 4,000 examples, four subsets, and
25 models. We use the inspected PDF for these method-specific claims and do not manufacture
an explanation for the discrepancy. The gold annotations for its main test are not simply an
unrestricted download. This study’s independent annotated corpus is therefore not a relabeling of
an assumed public 4,000-case resource.

4.3 Stream II: observability, accounting checks, and hallucinations

FinVerBench (Panda, 2026). This preprint studies numerical consistency of rendered financial
statements, with arithmetic, cross-statement, year-over-year, and magnitude perturbations. The
105-instance diagnostic subset excludes hidden-field positives that cannot be identified from the
shown evidence; it is not the size of the full corpus. Its rounding analysis demonstrates that
presentation and observability change the task [13].
The construction assumes an eligible set of financial concepts and source consistency. The inspected
methods exclude companies with source inconsistencies due to tagging variations or restatements,
and derive some missing fields from the very accounting identities later checked. This is useful for
controlled diagnostics but limits claims about natural correction conflicts. We keep its observability
discipline while admitting real revisions and retaining unresolved cases rather than forcing binary
labels.
AUDITFLOW (Wang et al., 2026). AUDITFLOW uses an executable symbolic environment
linking taxonomy relationships and filing facts, with deterministic tools and agent adjudication.
Its inspected v2 explicitly compares checker-only behavior with added agent judgment [14]. It
constrains any claim that putting a graph, calculator, and LLM together constitutes a new research
method.
The method assumes that represented accounting constraints and filing bindings cover the target
check. Its engine-recorded violation setting differs from an original-versus-corrected claim whose
label changes with the question’s authority policy. We adopt its lesson that deterministic checking
can explain most of the value. A complex agent is optional; the core test is whether a simple
policy-aware resolver contributes beyond an equally informed checker.
FAITH (Zhang et al., 2025). FAITH is a financial tabular-hallucination evaluation framework
[15]. The source dossier describes masked numerical spans from authentic reports; bibliographic
identity and venue are verified here, but the reported experimental effects are not reproduced. Its
central assumption is that the supplied table provides the target context. A missing or generated
number in one table differs from choosing between authentic numbers from different disclosure
states. We therefore avoid treating “financial numerical hallucination benchmark” as our novelty.
Combined implication. These papers already cover source-grounded arithmetic, traceability,
abstention, statement checks, and realistic numerical rendering. A defensible contribution must
specify a distinct estimand: the change in verification behavior when the same evidence is evaluated
under different, explicitly stated historical-authority policies. If the existing strong baselines are
already accurate on that estimand, the proposed method contribution disappears.

  Novelty constraint. “LLM + XBRL + calculator,” “detect wrong years,” “use a graph,”
  and “abstain when unsure” are rejected contribution claims. The proposal is an empirical
  boundary-condition study with an auditable benchmark.

4.5 Stream IV: retrieval and atomic factuality

T2 -RAGBench (Strich et al., 2026). This benchmark moves text/table questions beyond
oracle context into retrieval, with 23,088 question–context–answer triples in the oﬀicial record
[22]. It assumes that a context-independent question can identify the relevant evidence within the
corpus. Its limitation for our study is not a lack of retrieval complexity: finding both versions still
leaves an authority decision. We therefore report retrieval and verification as separate experimental
stages.
FinMRAGBench (Yang et al., 2026). Its oﬀicial record describes 887 expert-verified mul-
timodal QA cases requiring multi-page and multi-document evidence [23]. Its contribution is
realistic evidence integration. That does not establish an evaluation of paired original-as-filed and
historical-as-known truth conditions. Nevertheless, it rules out a generic “multiple documents make
financial QA hard” novelty claim. If its released cases already instantiate our full policy-switch
task, the scope and credit must be revised before preregistration.
FActScore (Min et al., 2023). FActScore decomposes long-form output into atomic facts
and assesses their support [26]. Its assumptions include a reliable knowledge source and suitable
fact decomposition. Financial calculation and filing authority may require more context than an
isolated sentence. We retain the discipline of judging individual claims, but do not introduce
atomic verification as a new idea.
RAGTruth (Niu et al., 2024). RAGTruth provides manually annotated hallucinations in nat-
urally generated retrieval-augmented responses [25]. It assumes that the supplied context defines
the grounding task and measures errors at response and word levels. Our question is upstream:
whether the selected authoritative context is appropriate. Its annotation practice motivates pre-
serving evidence spans and distinguishing generated claims from constructed evaluation questions.

4.6 Retrieval parity is necessary but not suﬀicient

Supplying every system the same documents eliminates one information-access advantage. It does
not eliminate an annotation advantage. Giving one method gold correction edges while another
must infer them would still be unfair. The protocol therefore has two explicitly separated tracks:
a resolver track with the same structured facts and edge annotations for all methods, and an
extraction track in which all methods receive the same raw filing excerpts and must recover those
fields.
An oracle-fact ablation will diagnose parsing failures but will never be reported as deployable
end-to-end accuracy. Candidate facts must be assembled without searching for a number equal to
the claimed answer, because such value-based retrieval can leak the answer into source selection.
We predeclare eligible concepts and source windows, randomize packet order, and preserve the
complete distractor policy.

4.7 Stream V: temporal semantics and ambiguous evidence

Temporal data management. Jensen and Snodgrass distinguish valid time from transaction
time and explain why historical database states require explicit representation [6]. Our reporting
interval is analogous to valid time; the filing’s public availability and the research system’s acqui-
sition time must be kept separately. This established framework undermines any assertion that
“as-of evidence” is itself a new theoretical concept. Our contribution is a financial verification
evaluation that applies the distinction carefully.
Temporal economic verification. Mori et al. translate natural-language economic claims into
temporal Datalog queries over time-series evidence [7]. They explicitly study economic claims and
temporal operators, not just generic news chronology. The approach assumes trusted time-series
facts and a template-supported logical interpretation. It does not establish a benchmark for con-
flicting versions of the same filed fact under distinct authority policies. This is a close predecessor
and must be discussed, not omitted because it predates LLM-based financial verification.
ChronoFact. Barik, Hsu, and Lee model relationships among events in claims and evidence,
including chronological order, overlapping events, and repeated events [8]. The inspected paper
constructs timelines and predicts supported, refuted, or insuﬀicient-evidence labels. Its assump-
tions concern event extraction and temporal alignment. It makes “temporal fact-checking” and
a three-way temporal verdict established prior art. The proposed filing task additionally distin-
guishes the economic period from disclosure availability and fact-scoped correction authority.
AmbiFC. Glockner et al. retain conflicting but valid evidence interpretations and study anno-
tation distributions rather than imposing one hard label everywhere [9]. This directly constrains
our treatment of ambiguity. Disagreement is not automatically annotator error, and adjudication
cannot manufacture certainty. We retain individual annotations and allow gold U or an explicitly
disputed item. A hard-label benchmark is only defensible for the prespecified policy and evidence
contract.
Selective prediction. Geifman and El-Yaniv study the trade-off between coverage and condi-
tional error [27]. Withholding decisions can improve error among decided cases, but an all-refusing
model has no meaningful conditional-risk estimate. We therefore separate gold underdetermina-
tion, system refusal, decisive coverage, and all-case accuracy. No distribution-free guarantee is
inferred for future filing populations from empirical risk curves.
Synthesis. The proposed task is a composition of established ideas. Its scientific value depends
on documenting a consequential failure mode not already captured by the closest benchmarks,
controlling evidence and authority information, and showing whether a minimal resolver addresses
that failure. The composition alone is not enough.

4.8 Stream VI: what the rejected credit and fraud ideas teach

Temporal model selection. Realistic fraud evaluation already addresses chronology, delayed
feedback, drift, and operations [32]. Bergmeir et al. show that nonchronological cross-validation
can be valid under specified time-series assumptions, so “random splits are always wrong” is
indefensible [33]. Recent temporal-fraud and capacity-constrained review studies further limit
novelty [35, 34]. P1’s reported three-window null pilot supports neither protocol superiority nor
financial savings. Its valuable contribution to this proposal is disciplined locking of evidence and
evaluation decisions.
Coalition explanations. Grouped Shapley attribution is established [36]; reciprocal rank fusion
is established [37]; BioMark already combines feature rankings using RRF [39]. Stow studies
SHAP/LIME disagreement, and SHAP-McNemar addresses feature selection in credit risk [38,
40]. Agreement is not evidence that the agreed explanation is correct. P3’s coalition-sum and
monotonicity safeguards are useful auditing lessons, but do not solve source authority in financial
documents.
Durable recourse. RobX explicitly targets robust counterfactuals for tree ensembles [41], and
probabilistically robust recourse studies cost–robustness trade-offs [42]. Jabbari’s 2026 work studies
threshold changes caused by competition even with a fixed scoring model [43]. A recourse paper
would need to separate model retraining, threshold policy, and feasible interventions. HELOC and
Taiwan do not supply the real longitudinal credit observations needed for the original temporal
claims. BAF has synthetic month structure and raises distinct fraud-evasion concerns [31].
Conformal claims. The alleged “Johansson et al. 2026” title could not be verified. Maalej,
Sönströd, and Johansson’s actual 2025 paper concerns counterfactual explanations for conformal
prediction sets [44]. It does not justify a guarantee that selected recourse survives future retraining
under drift. A finite-sample quantile formula cannot substitute for exchangeability and selection
assumptions.
Decision support versus causal advice. Barocas et al. analyze the assumptions separating
principal reasons from counterfactual explanations; Karimi et al. distinguish feature changes from
causal interventions [45, 46]. This proposal avoids an unvalidated promise of what a person should
change to receive credit. It offers evidence-linked verification of public corporate disclosures, with
a clear boundary around its claims.
These streams were audited with the same standard as the chosen direction. Their rejection is
based on prior-art overlap, data mismatch, and scope, not on the assumption that financial NLP
is automatically more novel.

5. Research gaps

The following are bounded gaps in the reviewed evidence, not claims that an entire research field
has ignored time, uncertainty, or accounting. Each gap has an observable closure condition. Dis-
covering prior work that satisfies that condition reduces the novelty claim.

5.1 G1: authority-conditioned evaluation of the same facts

FinGround explicitly chooses the latest filing for conflicting restated figures, while VeriFin already
grounds numerical constraints in filing evidence [11, 10]. Neither inspected version establishes a
paired evaluation in which an unchanged packet is queried about its original filing, its state at
a historical cutoff, and its corrected state. ChronoFact supplies temporal reasoning but targets
event chronology [8]. The specific gap is evidence about when a latest-value convention is an
inappropriate interpretation of the requested claim.
Closure condition: an openly inspectable benchmark with authentic correction relationships,
explicit authority policies, paired claims, and separate results for legitimate verdict flips and policy-
invariant controls. If an existing supplement meets this condition, use it as prior art or a replication
target.

5.2 G2: scope-sensitive correction provenance

An amendment’s existence does not, by itself, identify every numerical fact it changes. SEC
APIs expose accessions and contexts, but aggregated facts have documented scope limits [1].
FinVerBench’s exclusions of inconsistent source facts and constructed accounting identities show
why source reconciliation can affect what a benchmark measures [13]. The gap is a transparent
evaluation of fact-scoped replacement, including unchanged line items and unresolved conflicts.
Closure condition: every replacement is attached to an exact fact key and documentary span;
evaluation distinguishes source extraction failures, incorrect replacement scope, and verdict rea-
soning. A newest-document lookup alone does not close this gap.

5.3 G3: useful uncertainty under authority ambiguity

AmbiFC demonstrates that interpretation ambiguity matters, and selective prediction formalizes
the coverage–risk tradeoff [9, 27]. Existing financial verifiers also abstain [10, 11]. The remaining
question is whether uncertainty is appropriately localized to ambiguous authority, rather than used
to hide resolvable errors. This is an evaluation gap, not an invention of abstention.
Closure condition: ground-truth underdetermination is distinguished from a system’s refusal;
all-case and coverage-conditioned metrics are both reported, including zero-coverage behavior and
unsupported-claim acceptance.

5.4 G4: evidence for a minimal intervention

AUDITFLOW’s checker-versus-agent distinction and financial retrieval benchmarks caution
against attributing every gain to an elaborate reasoning architecture [14, 22, 23]. The gap is
whether a small policy/context resolver adds anything beyond a well-specified structured baseline
on authentic correction cases.
Closure condition: equal evidence, equal policy instructions, a strong simple baseline, an inde-
pendent policy-aware control, issuer-disjoint testing, and publication of a tie or loss. A numerical
improvement over an uninformed number matcher is insuﬀicient.

7.2 A fact-scoped authority graph

Let G = (F, E) be a directed graph whose nodes are evidence facts. An edge (fi , fj ) ∈ E states that
fj explicitly corrects or replaces fi for the same fact key, with an attached justification span and
reviewer decision. Require t(fi ) < t(fj ), except that a documented within-submission ordering may
be used with an explicit tie rule. A cycle is a validation failure, not a silently repaired chronology.
For as-known policies, first restrict to Fa = {f ∈ F : t(f ) ≤ a} and the edges whose endpoints
are in Fa . Then remove a fact from the active set only when a valid outgoing correction path
establishes an eligible replacement for that key. Multiple incomparable surviving values induce
multiple interpretations. Unchanged facts remain active even if their document has an amendment.
A later comparative table can supply a documented correction, but its later timestamp alone does
not prove replacement.
For original-as-filed, restrict to the named accession and its original context; use its reported value
even when a later corrected value is known. The benchmark thereby distinguishes a report-about-a-
report from a claim about corrected accounts. Explicit withdrawals, accounting-definition changes,
and scope changes are separately annotated; they are not forced into same-key replacement edges.

Proposition 1 (Cutoff invariance). Assume fact eligibility, key mappings, and authority edges are
derived only from eligible evidence. Adding facts whose public-availability time exceeds a does not
change the verdict under a fixed as-known policy at a.

Proof sketch. Future facts are removed before interpretation construction. The eligible fact set and
its induced correction graph therefore remain unchanged. The admitted interpretations, intervals,
and the verdict rule remain unchanged. If a mapping or correction annotation itself uses a future
note, the premise fails. This is why annotation provenance also needs an availability time. □

Proposition 2 (Conditional decisive soundness). Suppose the true intended interpretation belongs
to nonempty Ω, every interval Iω contains the corresponding value, and J(c) correctly represents
the claim. Then output S implies that value lies in J(c), and output C implies it lies outside J(c).

Proof. Both conclusions follow by applying the universal condition in the verdict rule to the true
interpretation. □
Limits of the propositions. These are specification checks, not statistical guarantees. An
omitted interpretation, erroneous unit conversion, wrong scope mapping, or fabricated correction
edge can invalidate the assumptions. A new eligible correction can remove an old interpretation
and legitimately change a verdict. There is no unconditional monotonicity of truth as evidence
grows, no conformal coverage claim, and no claim that all authority graphs can be recovered
automatically.

7.3 Precision, arithmetic, and worked examples

For a decimal value v reported to d decimal places in its declared unit, use the conservative
rounding interval
                              I(v, d) = [v − 12 10−d , v + 12 10−d ].
Negative d denotes rounding to powers of ten. Exact-valued disclosures can use a singleton only
when exactness is justified. An inline-XBRL scale multiplier is applied before conversion to canon-
ical units; do not mistake the displayed scale for the decimals attribute. Endpoint inclusion is
deliberately conservative. The reporting note takes precedence when it specifies truncation or
another rounding rule.
For a claim qc rounded to step rc > 0, set J(c) = [qc − rc /2, qc + rc /2]. For an explicitly exact
claim use J(c) = {qc }. For a claim with a directional comparator, use the corresponding half-
line and a declared boundary convention. The primary benchmark covers equality, difference, and
percentage-change claims with explicit rounding; vague qualifiers such as “approximately doubled”
are excluded unless their tolerance is stated.
For operand intervals [l1 , u1 ] and [l2 , u2 ], subtraction yields [l1 − u2 , u1 − l2 ]. For division, if
0 ∈/ [l2 , u2 ], take the minimum and maximum of the four endpoint ratios. If the denominator
interval contains zero, the ratio is unresolved. Interval arithmetic can overestimate uncertainty
when operands share dependencies; preserve shared operands where exact symbolic cancellation is
justified, or record conservative U rather than tighten bounds arbitrarily.

8.2 Splits, annotation, and leakage controls

Issuer-disjoint split. Assign a whole issuer, all accessions, and all correction families to one
partition before generating claims. Near-duplicate tables, subsidiary/parent overlaps, and shared
merger histories are flagged; related entities are grouped when their evidence is substantially
shared. Freeze the split manifest and its hash. Group stratification by sector and correction type
is a balance aid, not evidence of independent sampling.
Time is a task input, not automatically a prospective split. Each claim’s cutoff limits its
eligible evidence and annotations. The main experiment is retrospective with issuer holdout; it is
not a deployment forecast. A later-period stress slice can test date sensitivity, but overlapping slices
are not independent retraining windows. A pretrained language model may have seen public filings
before this study. Evidence deletion and deliberately changed-context controls probe reliance on
memorized facts; they do not prove absence of pretraining contamination [28].
Annotation protocol. Two reviewers independently mark each claim’s exact context, eligible
interpretations, correction links, numerical expression, precision, verdict, and evidence span. At
least one reviewer needs financial-reporting competence. Hide system predictions from both. Pre-
serve first-pass labels and disagreement reasons before adjudication. Report raw agreement and
label-wise confusion; any chance-corrected coeﬀicient is supplementary because class balance and
structured partial agreement matter. Adjudicated labels are the benchmark’s operational standard,
not infallible truth.

9.3 Statistical testing and sample adequacy

Independence assumption. Test issuers are treated as approximately independent units for
inference; claims, correction families, seeds, and repeated model calls within an issuer are not
independent replicates. Shared sectors, accounting rules, and macroeconomic shocks can weaken
this assumption. Report sector-block sensitivity and descriptive results when there are too few
blocks. This is an inference about the constructed issuer population under stated assumptions,
not all future filings [29].
Let Dg = eB4,g − eP,g . For the primary exact two-sided sign test, discard ties and let n be the
number of nonzero issuer differences and W the number with Dg > 0. Under the null Pr(Dg >
0 | Dg ̸= 0) = 1/2 and independence, W ∼ Binomial(n, 1/2). The test addresses consistency of
direction across issuers, not directly equality of mean error. Report ∆, its issuer-bootstrap 95%
interval, and the sign-test result as distinct summaries. Bootstrap intervals are approximate with
40 purposively selected clusters.
Multiplicity. If H1–H3 receive formal tests, sort their valid two-sided p-values as p(1) ≤ p(2) ≤ p(3)
and apply Holm thresholds 0.05/(4 − j) at step j ∈ {1, 2, 3} [30]. H2 uses the same sign procedure
on issuer-level errors within the authority-sensitive stratum. H3 uses issuer-level selective-risk
differences only if both methods have nonzero decisive answers for every issuer and overall achieved
coverage lies within the preregistered 0.75–0.85 band. Otherwise H3 is descriptive and is assigned
p = 1 for the planned family, without changing the other thresholds. Report the coverage mismatch
even when the condition is met.
Minimum units are not adequate power. The smallest attainable two-sided exact sign-test
p-value is 2/2n . For M planned comparisons, even the most favorable data cannot cross the first
Holm threshold unless                     ⌈     (      )⌉

10. Expected contributions and honest novelty claim

  Defensible claim. The study would provide an auditable evaluation of financial verifi-
  cation under explicit historical and correction-authority policies, using authentic disclosure
  relationships, paired claims, and equal-evidence baselines. It would test whether fact-scoped
  authority handling improves over strong simple rules. The review does not establish a new
  general verification algorithm or the first use of time, XBRL, arithmetic, provenance, or
  abstention in financial NLP.


10.1 Contributions conditional on completing the study

C1: an evaluation object. A curated set of original/corrective disclosure families, paired policy-
conditioned claims, source spans, and explicit uncertainty labels. Its value depends on authentic
provenance and the absence of answer leakage. Merely changing dates in synthetic templates is
not enough.
C2: a precise task contract. A distinction between reporting period, public availability, au-
thority policy, and acquisition time; fact-scoped replacement; conservative numerical precision;
and separate gold uncertainty versus operational refusal. These formalize the evaluation and ex-
pose failure cases. They are not advertised as a novel theorem beyond the elementary propositions
in Section 7.
C3: evidence about minimal suﬀiciency. A comparison of exact-context checks, cutoff-aware
latest selection, scoped authority resolution, and an independent full-policy control. A policy-
aware prompt may be suﬀicient. A transparent reference resolver may be useful without being
superior to every modern verifier.
C4: a reusable error analysis. Distinguish extraction, context, time eligibility, replacement
scope, arithmetic, and uncertainty errors. Categories may overlap; a label and a mechanism are
not interchangeable. The output is a diagnostic account of failure, not a single leaderboard number
stripped of coverage and provenance.

10.2 What would undermine the claim

An existing released benchmark with equivalent authentic authority-switch cases would reduce C1
to replication or extension. FinGround’s explicit restatement handling already narrows any broad
“first period-aware financial verifier” claim [11]; VeriFin and AUDITFLOW narrow claims about
symbolic checking [10, 14]. If B4 or the independent policy-aware control matches P, C3 becomes
evidence that simple specification suﬀices. If only gold-parsed Track A succeeds, the contribution
is a controlled benchmark, not a working autonomous filing auditor.
Venue fit. The likely manuscript should be framed for financial NLP evaluation, AI in finance,
or a benchmark/resource track. It needs credible annotation, accessible evidence, and informative
strong baselines. A general machine-learning theory venue would be a poor fit for the present
elementary propositions. No venue acceptance or impact level is promised.
Attribution. P2 supplies the selected problem family; P1 supplies temporal leakage discipline and
the requirement to test simple alternatives; P3 supplies caution about mathematical guarantees,
data semantics, and inadequate independent samples. The final study does not inherit the sources’
unexecuted performance expectations.

14. Ethics, fairness, and regulatory considerations

Research scope. This is a proposed study of public corporate disclosures. It does not decide
credit eligibility, recommend trades, or supply borrower recourse. A verdict is evidence-relative
and can be wrong. Release examples with their provenance, authority policy, and limitations so
that a decontextualized “false” label is not mistaken for an allegation of corporate misconduct.
ECOA / Regulation B. The source documents’ four-feature compliance rationale is rejected.
Regulation B requires specific principal reasons in its applicable adverse-action framework, or
prescribed notice of the right to receive them; its oﬀicial interpretation does not fix the number.
Sex and marital status, age, income source, and related variables have specific restrictions and
exceptions in 12 CFR 1002.6. A broad claim that every use of age is prohibited is inaccurate.
A public Taiwan dataset is not automatically governed by US law [3]. These provisions do not
require this corporate-verification method.
FCRA. Relevant adverse-action notices and score disclosures depend on the statutory trigger.
The score-factor limit is generally four, or five if inquiries are a key factor; it is distinct from a
creditor’s principal reasons. Neither a feature-importance ranking nor an algorithmic action plan
automatically satisfies these duties [4, 3, 45]. The final proposal does not claim legal compliance
from explanation length.
EU AI Act. Annex III 5(b) identifies natural-person creditworthiness/credit-score systems as
a high-risk use case, except systems used to detect financial fraud. The exception is not an
exemption from the entire Act. Article 86 concerns explanations of certain consequential decisions
based on covered high-risk outputs; it is not a general right to a successful counterfactual action.
Article 2’s research exclusions have conditions and do not justify treating every real-world research
deployment as exempt [5].

  Dated legal-source conflict. On 23 October 2026, the retrieved Commission AI Act Service
  Desk stated that its text reflected a consolidated version of 27 July 2026, and displayed 2

Fairness and representation. Report performance by sector, issuer size, document complexity,
and correction type where denominators permit. These are coverage diagnostics, not a substi-
tute for a protected-class fairness analysis in a future credit-decision application. Do not infer
equal treatment of borrowers from good financial-document accuracy. Avoid collecting personal
identifiers, honor source rights, and document any model/API transfer of evidence.
Release and accountability. Use an institutional ethics review if locally required for the even-
tual study; no such approval is represented as already obtained. Provide a correction process
for annotation disputes, preserve source versions, and explain that public filings and automated
interpretations can both contain errors. The research output supports inspection, not regulatory
certification.

B.1 Why the alternatives lose

Temporal selection regret is the strongest fallback. P1’s question is falsifiable and compu-
tationally modest. Its diﬀiculty is establishing a contribution beyond realistic stream evaluation
and known leakage controls [32, 33]. A new study needs enough independent deployment units,
a fixed candidate-training contract, and defensible operational loss assumptions. The supplied
three-window null is a reason to remain cautious, not a reason to suppress the topic. It loses to
the selected direction because the latter offers a clearer source-level annotation object and sharper
interpretation controls.
Robust review allocation has an identification problem. P1’s budgeted allocation idea
could matter operationally, but public fraud labels do not establish investigation costs, recoverable
loss, or how review changes outcomes. Top-K cost-sensitive screening already provides close prior
art [34]. A paper driven mostly by assumed cost ratios risks reporting preferences built into those
assumptions. It becomes stronger with real operational feedback, which is outside the present
public-data budget.
Focused coalition selection is feasible but incremental. Removing recourse would make P3
easier to test. Yet feature grouping, RRF, and explanation disagreement are established [36, 37,
39, 38]. A high agreement score could reflect shared bias, fewer items, or a common background
distribution. The study would need an independently justified fidelity target and careful null
controls. It is a reasonable second fallback, but does not currently promise a clearer contribution
than authority-conditioned verification.
Full COMET and the all-in-one merger are over-scoped. Compact prediction, explanation
stability, monotonicity, actionability, and future recourse validity are separate scientific claims.
Combining them raises annotation, causal, temporal, and ethical requirements without a single
clean estimand. Robust recourse already has strong baselines [41, 42]; feature edits alone do not
establish feasible interventions [46]. The selected synthesis takes methodological safeguards, not
additional modules.
COST-RRF and synthetic generators remain diagnostics. Reviving rank fusion without a
new estimand has little novelty after established RRF and BioMark. A controlled generator can
expose failure mechanisms but cannot by itself establish applicability to real financial workflows.
Neither is discarded as a tool; neither becomes the final publication claim.
